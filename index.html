The year the gain is real but the balance still falls

A $1M portfolio withdrawing $50,000 a year, 8 percent a year for 13 of 15 years, can end at $380,234 or at $941,261. The two bad years landed first in the first life and last in the second. Ordering does all the damage. Same returns, same withdrawals, a $561,027 difference that is pure sequence.

I put the two bad years first on purpose. The 30 percent year takes the balance to $700,000, the $50,000 draw pulls it to $650,000, and the next year sheds another 10 percent before its draw, down to $535,000. Then recovery percent meets recovery dollars. Up 8 percent on $535,000 is $42,800 of gain against a $50,000 draw, so the balance falls another $7,200 in a year the market gained. Thirteen straight gaining years from there still leave $380,234, below the $625,000 the draw needs at 8 percent.

Reversed, with the 13 gaining years first, the same 15 returns end at $941,261. The 30 percent year still comes, landing on the $1,644,859 year-13 balance and erasing $493,458 in a single year; that crash year still closes above $1.1M, and even after the second bad year the portfolio sits at $941,261, where coverage is back: 8 percent of $941,261 is $75,301, more than the draw. Total drawn over 15 years: $750,000 in both lives. Order is what separates them.

Nothing here is forecast. The returns are a fixed list used twice, so the only moving part is the calendar. A spreadsheet retells the whole story in ten minutes: balance, gain, draw, one row per year, both orders side by side.
